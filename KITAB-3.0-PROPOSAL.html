Kitab 3.0
from tracker to reading memory

Kitab v2 is a very good reading tracker. It knows what you read, when, and how much you liked it. What it does not yet do is hold on to what the reading meant. Your Kindle highlights, your reviews, your ratings and your dates all live in separate rooms of the app. 3.0 puts them in one room, gives the app four tabs instead of eight destinations, and adds three features that no other tracker does well.

In briefSummary

What I am proposing

  1. Four tabs: Home, Shelves, Highlights, You. Library and TBR merge into Shelves. Discover and Rank stop being destinations and become actions inside the shelves and the year view. Settings folds into You.
  2. The book page becomes the hero screen. Cover-forward, one row for status and rating, then a timeline of everything you did with the book.
  3. Adding a book takes one tap. Search, tap, done. Editing is a sheet you open only if you want to.
  4. Real dates. Finish dates get optional day precision and start dates are recorded automatically. This unlocks pace, a reading calendar and an honest "days to finish".
  5. Eleven features get simplified, demoted or removed, including the spoiler toggle, the sidebar tag shortcuts, five notification toggles, the Enrich review list and the month-only date rule.
  6. Three bets: the Commonplace Book (a per-book journal that unifies highlights, notes, progress and review), Ask Your Library (questions answered from your own highlights, with citations), and Reading Circle (quiet sharing with family, no feed).
  7. Twelve to sixteen weeks of evenings, in five phases, each of which ships something usable on its own.

AssessmentWhere Kitab stands today

This is written from the code, not from memory. I read every screen, hook and API route while doing the v2.12 cleanup, so the list below is what the app actually does, including the parts that are quietly excellent and the parts that have accreted.

What is genuinely strong

  • Kindle highlight sync is a moat. Nobody else scrapes read.amazon.com through a logged-in WKWebView, incrementally, overnight, with a foreground fallback. It is the single most valuable thing in the app.
  • Capture is frictionless where it matters. Barcode scanning, the iOS share extension that resolves Amazon and Goodreads links, and Hardcover-first search all get a book into the app in seconds.
  • Discover is honest. Gemini recommendations are verified against Google Books, deduplicated against the library and past sessions, and never invent titles.
  • The foundations are right. Offline-first cache, dark mode by default, Playfair and DM Sans, teal on paper, Lucide icons, sheets for quick actions, haptics. The taste is already there.
  • It is fast now. After 3.0's prerequisite cleanup the main bundle is 668 KB, edits apply instantly and the library grid no longer mounts a hidden form per card.

Where it has accreted

  • Eight destinations for a personal app. Five bottom tabs plus an eight-item sidebar, with Rank and Discover as peers of Library.
  • Home does seven jobs. Goal, highlight of the day, currently reading, four stat tiles, anniversaries, recently read, and a Kindle sync card. Settings has nine cards.
  • TBR is invisible from Library. The Library filters out status = tbr, so a book you own lives in one of two places depending on whether you have started it.
  • Month-only finish dates. date_finished is always the first of the month and a start date is never written. That is why "average days to finish" had to be removed and why a reading calendar is impossible today.
  • Two modals to add a book. Search modal, then a two-tab form modal, before anything is saved.
  • Six copies of the status labels and colours, which disagree: "Finished" here, "Read" there; an amber TBR pill next to a blue TBR dot.
  • Four surfaces for the highlight of the day that pick three different quotes, because each seeds its own randomness.
  • Five notification toggles and two time pickers for a single user.

North starFive principles for 3.0

A memory, not a metric

Kitab exists to remember what you read and what it meant to you. Numbers are a by-product. No streaks, no guilt, no red badges when you have not opened a book this week.

Cover first, chrome second

Book covers are the most beautiful asset the app has. Screens should lead with them and let the interface recede, the way a good bookshop does.

One way to do each thing

One add flow, one status vocabulary, one daily highlight, one place for settings. Where two paths exist today, keep the better one.

Phone first, desktop faithful

Kitab lives on the iPhone home screen. Every screen is designed at 390 points wide first. Desktop gets the same screens with more room, not different screens.

Quiet by default

The app speaks up twice a day at most: a reading nudge you chose and a highlight you saved. Everything else waits on Home until you look.

  • Settings in five groups: Kindle (auto-sync, sync now, full re-sync, unmatched), Notifications (two toggles), Library tools (import, export, fill in missing details), Links (Libby), Account.

The gamechangersThree bets, ranked

Each of these changes what Kitab is, not just what it does. They are ranked by how much they deepen the core job, remembering what you read, and by how much they depend on things already in the app. The first two are pure upside. The third changes the app's nature and needs your decision.

1Highest confidence

The Commonplace Book

Every book gets a journal. Every journal is searchable.

A commonplace book is the centuries-old habit of copying passages you want to keep into one notebook, with your own thoughts beside them. Kitab already has half of one: it syncs your Kindle highlights. What it lacks is the other half, your thoughts, and a place where both sit together in time.

What it is. On every book page, a timeline: the day you started, the pages you logged, each highlight as it arrived, notes you typed in the moment, the day you finished, your rating, your review. A global Journal view on the Highlights tab shows the same entries across all books by date, so "what was I reading and thinking in March" has an answer.

Why it is gamechanging. It turns four separate features (highlights, review, progress, dates) into one story per book, which is what people actually want to look back on. It gives paper books the same treatment as Kindle books. And it is the raw material for the other two bets: Ask reads the journal, and Year in Books quotes it.

How it works

  1. Book page shows the timeline below the control row. A field at the bottom reads "Add a note…".
  2. Logging pages from Home writes a progress entry. Changing status writes a status entry. Kindle sync writes highlight entries with the sync date.
  3. A note can be attached to a highlight (reply) or stand alone with an optional page number.
  4. Finishing a book offers three prompts: "One sentence for future you", "Who would you give this to", "Rate it". Skippable.
  5. Highlights tab gains a Journal segment: everything, by date, with the book cover as the marker.

Under the hood

  • New journal_entries table: book_id, kind (note, progress, status, review, finished), body, page, highlight_id nullable, created_at.
  • Highlights stay in highlights; the timeline is a union of both, ordered by date.
  • Typed highlights are rows in highlights with source = 'manual'.
  • Offline queue is built here, since notes and pages must work without signal.

Cost and risk

Three to four weeks. Scope is the risk; ship notes and page logging first, prompts and the Journal segment second. No new services.

2Highest leverage

Ask Your Library

Questions answered from your own highlights, with citations.

You have synced hundreds, soon thousands, of highlights. Today the only way back to one is to remember which book it was in. Ask makes the whole collection answerable.

What it is. A question box on the Highlights tab. "What did I save about patience?" "Which of my books disagree with each other about money?" "Summarise what I highlighted in Sapiens." Every answer quotes your highlights and links to the book and the passage. On any single highlight, a Connections row shows the three most related highlights from other books.

Why it is gamechanging. It is the payoff for the moat. The Kindle sync becomes worth doing not because the highlights are stored but because they come back when you need them. No mainstream reading tracker does this over the reader's own text, with citations, for free.

How it works

  1. Each highlight, note and review gets an embedding when it is created or synced.
  2. A question is embedded, the closest twenty passages are fetched, and Gemini writes an answer that may only use those passages and must cite them.
  3. Answers render as prose with numbered citations; tapping one opens the book page scrolled to the highlight.
  4. Connections on a highlight is the same nearest-neighbour query with no model call at all.
  5. Four suggested questions appear when the box is empty, generated from your most-highlighted books.

Under the hood

  • Supabase pgvector extension (available on the free tier) and a highlight_embeddings table.
  • Gemini's embedding model and Gemini 3.5 Flash for answers, both on the free tier already used by Discover. Both calls go through a serverless route with the same session check as /api/recommend.
  • Backfill embeds the existing highlights once, in batches, from the app.

Cost and risk

Two to three weeks. Near-zero running cost at personal scale. The risk is a confident wrong answer; the mitigation is that the model is only shown your passages and every sentence carries a citation. If it cannot answer from your highlights, it says so.

3Needs a decision

Reading Circle

Quiet sharing with the people you actually talk to about books.

Kitab's handoff notes say it is a personal tool, not a product. Your own brief says you build for yourself, friends and family. Both are true, and the gap between them is where this bet lives.

What it is. Invite up to a handful of people. Each keeps their own Kitab. A Circle view under You shows what each person is reading now and finished recently, covers only. You can pass a book to someone: it lands on their To read shelf with a note from you. A shared shelf ("Summer reads", "Kids") lets everyone add. There is no feed, no likes, no comments beyond the note, and reviews and highlights are never visible unless you share one on purpose.

Why it is gamechanging. Reading is one of the few things families talk about across generations, and Kitab has no way to carry that conversation. Children's reading in particular has nowhere to live today. A circle makes the app a family thing without making it a social network.

How it works

  1. You › Circle › Invite generates a link. The invitee signs in with Google as usual and lands in the circle.
  2. The Circle view lists members with their current book and last three finished.
  3. Long-press any book anywhere for "Pass to…", pick a person, add a note.
  4. Passed books arrive on the To read shelf with the sender's name and note in the book's journal.
  5. Leaving the circle removes your rows from everyone's view immediately.

Under the hood

  • Tables: circles, circle_members, circle_shelves, passed_books.
  • A database view exposes only title, author, cover, status and finish date of members' books. Row-level security keeps reviews, highlights and journals private.
  • Invites are single-use tokens with a seven-day expiry.

Cost and risk

Three to four weeks. This is the only bet that adds a second user to the data model, so the security work has to be done carefully. It is also the only one that changes what the app is for, which is why it is ranked third and shipped last.

On the third bet. If you would rather keep Kitab entirely personal, say so and the roadmap ends at Phase 3. Nothing in the first two bets depends on it.

Runner-up: Year in Books

An annual recap in the style of a music service's year-end story was the fourth candidate. It is a genuine delight and it is in the improvements table above with a size of five evenings. It did not make the top three because it deepens the existing product rather than changing it, and because it gets far better once the Commonplace Book exists to quote from.

HonestyRisks and what I would cut first

  • The navigation change is the one people feel. Merging Library and TBR moves muscle memory. Mitigation: the To read shelf is the default when you have books there, and the tab bar keeps the same icons for Home and Shelves.
  • Real dates change a rule that was chosen deliberately. Month-only was picked so that logging a finished book never required remembering the day. The proposal keeps that: the day is optional and defaults to today, and the edit sheet still shows month and year first.
  • Ask can be confidently wrong. Citations are mandatory and the model never sees anything but your own passages. Even so, the answer box says "from your highlights" and never "the book says".
  • Scope. Every phase has a first half that ships alone. If time is short, the cut order is: Circle, then quote cards and widgets, then Year in Books, then the Journal segment (keep the per-book timeline).
  • The Kindle scraper is fragile by nature. Nothing here touches it, and the Commonplace Book makes the app valuable even when Amazon changes its markup, because typed highlights and notes do not depend on it.

MeasureWhat success looks like

For a personal app the honest measure is a feeling: do you reach for Kitab when you close a book. A few numbers will tell us whether the feeling is being earned.

  • Adding a book takes under eight seconds from opening the app, including a barcode scan.
  • Every book finished after 3.0 has a date within a day of the real one, and most have a note.
  • You open the Highlights tab more days than not, because there is something new or something asked there.
  • Ask answers at least half of your questions from your own highlights without saying it cannot.
  • The January recap gets opened, and shared with at least one person.
  • Nothing in the app makes you feel behind.
